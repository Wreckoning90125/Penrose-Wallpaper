# Discrete subgroups of I(Hⁿ) and hyperbolic tilings

A *tiling* of Hⁿ that comes from a single fundamental domain copied around
is exactly the orbit of that domain under a discrete subgroup of I(Hⁿ).
Discrete + 2-D = a **Fuchsian group**; discrete + 3-D = a **Kleinian
group**. This file collects the structural facts that drive what is and
what is not realisable as a renderable hyperbolic tiling.

The Euclidean engine in this repo deals with substitution and
cut-and-project tilings of E²; the discrete-group framing only becomes
necessary when we move to H², because there is no longer a flat
translational symmetry to use as the structure-of-record.

## What "discrete" means here

A topological group Γ is **discrete** if {1} is open. For a subgroup Γ of
I(Hⁿ) — equivalently M(Bⁿ) or M(Uⁿ) — discreteness is the same as the
following geometric condition (Ratcliffe Theorem 5.3.5):

> Γ acts **discontinuously** on Hⁿ: for each compact K ⊂ Hⁿ, the set
> {g ∈ Γ : gK ∩ K ≠ ∅} is finite.

Equivalently again, every orbit of Γ in Hⁿ is a closed discrete subset of
Hⁿ. This is the property that makes "the tiles do not pile up" precise.

A discrete subgroup of I(Hⁿ) is automatically *countable*. A finite
subgroup is automatically discrete; an infinite discrete subgroup
necessarily has an infinite orbit.

## Elementary subgroups

A subgroup G of I(Hⁿ) is **elementary** if some orbit of G in the closed
ball B̄ⁿ is finite. Equivalently (Ratcliffe Theorem 5.5.9 for discrete G),
G has a free abelian subgroup of finite index. Elementary groups are
small; the interesting tilings come from *non-elementary* discrete groups.

Three flavours, mutually exclusive:

| Type | Definition | Discrete groups of this type |
|---|---|---|
| **Elliptic type** | G has a finite orbit in **open** Bⁿ | G fixes a point of Bⁿ; conjugate to a subgroup of O(n). Discrete ⇒ finite. |
| **Parabolic type** | G fixes a unique point of Sⁿ⁻¹ and has no finite orbit in Bⁿ | conjugate to an infinite discrete subgroup of I(Eⁿ⁻¹). Acts as "translations along horospheres." |
| **Hyperbolic type** | neither of the above | conjugate to a subgroup of S(Eⁿ⁻¹)⋆ that does not fix any point of the positive nth axis; contains an infinite cyclic subgroup of finite index generated by a hyperbolic translation. |

A subgroup whose every orbit is infinite — equivalently, no fixed point on
the boundary — is **non-elementary**. Every infinite Fuchsian group with
fundamental domain of finite area is non-elementary.

## Fuchsian groups (n = 2)

A **Fuchsian group** is a discrete subgroup of I(H²) — equivalently, of
PSL(2,ℝ) acting on U², or of PSU(1,1) acting on B² (Poincaré disk).
Historically: Poincaré called any discrete subgroup of PSL(2,ℝ) leaving a
circle invariant a Fuchsian group, motivated by automorphic functions
satisfying f(γz) = f(z).

A Fuchsian group Γ has a **fundamental domain** F ⊂ H² — an open subset
such that the translates {γF : γ ∈ Γ} tile H² without overlap, and the
union of closures covers H². For finitely generated non-elementary Γ, F is
a hyperbolic polygon (Dirichlet construction: pick a base point p ∉ orbit
of any non-identity element; F is the set of points closer to p than to
any other translate γp).

The **signature** of a finitely generated Fuchsian group records the
genus g of the quotient surface H²/Γ, the orders of conical singularities
(elliptic fixed points), and the number of cusps (parabolic fixed points):
(g; m₁, …, m_r; s). The fundamental-domain area is the **Gauss–Bonnet
sum**:

    Area(H²/Γ) = 2π [(2g − 2) + s + Σ (1 − 1/mᵢ)].

A signature is realisable as a Fuchsian group exactly when this sum is
positive (Poincaré's polygon theorem).

## Regular tilings {p, q}

A **regular tiling {p, q}** is the orbit of a regular p-gon, joined q to a
vertex, under the discrete group it generates. The model carrying it is
determined by 1/p + 1/q:

| 1/p + 1/q | space | examples |
|---|---|---|
| > 1/2 | sphere S² | {3,3}, {3,4}, {3,5}, {4,3}, {5,3} — the five Platonic solids |
| = 1/2 | E² | {3,6}, {4,4}, {6,3} — equilateral-triangle, square, hexagonal tilings |
| < 1/2 | H² | {5,4}, {7,3}, {5,6}, {3,7}, …, {∞, ∞} — infinitely many |

The group generated by the reflections in the sides of a fundamental
right-triangle of angles π/p, π/q, π/2 is a discrete group of isometries
of the appropriate model space (Coxeter's reflection group construction).
The orientation-preserving index-2 subgroup is the symmetry group of the
tiling proper.

**Coxeter's *Circle Limit* tilings (the source of Escher's prints) are
exactly orbit pictures of {p, q} for 1/p + 1/q < 1/2**, drawn in the
Poincaré disk.

## Discrete subgroups of I(Eⁿ): Bieberbach

For contrast — and because parabolic-type discrete subgroups of I(Hⁿ) are
discrete subgroups of I(Eⁿ⁻¹) under the Poincaré-extension identification
— here are the corresponding Euclidean facts (Ratcliffe §5.4):

- A discrete subgroup of ℝⁿ is generated by a set of linearly independent
  vectors — a **lattice** of rank ≤ n.
- A discrete subgroup of I(Eⁿ) has an abelian normal subgroup of finite
  index that contains every translation. The index is bounded by a constant
  k_n(1/2) depending only on n.
- The abelian normal subgroup decomposes as K ⊕ H where K is a finite
  group acting trivially on a flat m-plane P, and H is a free abelian
  group of rank m acting on P by translation.

Bieberbach's theorem says the discrete cocompact subgroups of I(Eⁿ) (the
*Euclidean crystallographic groups*) are exactly the 17 wallpaper groups
in n = 2 and the 230 space groups in n = 3. There is no analogous finite
list for H² — there are infinitely many Fuchsian-group signatures.

## Building fundamental polygons: Dirichlet domains

Given a discontinuous group Γ acting on X (X = Sⁿ, Eⁿ, Hⁿ), the
**Dirichlet domain** D(a) centred at a basepoint a (which must not be
fixed by any non-identity element of Γ — Ratcliffe Theorem 6.6.12
guarantees one exists in any rigid X) is

    D(a) = ∩_{g ≠ 1 in Γ} H_g(a),     H_g(a) = {x : d(x,a) < d(x,ga)}

i.e. the set of points strictly closer to a than to any other point of
the orbit Γa. Equivalently, `x ∈ D(a) ⇔ x is a nearest point of Γx to a`.
For X geodesically connected + complete + finitely compact (true for our
three model spaces), D(a) is a convex, locally finite **fundamental
domain** for Γ — exactly the polygon to render with (Ratcliffe Theorems
6.6.13, 6.7.1).

H_g(a) is the open half-space bounded by the perpendicular bisector of
the geodesic segment [a, ga]. In B² this is a half-disk bounded by an
arc orthogonal to S¹. The Dirichlet polygon is the intersection of
finitely many of these (only the close-enough ga contribute sides).

## Side-pairings, cycles, and the group presentation

Let P be an exact convex fundamental polygon for Γ. For each side S of P:

- There is a unique element **g_S ∈ Γ** with `S = P ∩ g_S(P)`. The side
  `S' = g_S⁻¹(S)` is also a side of P, and `g_{S'} = g_S⁻¹`. The set
  `Φ = {g_S : S a side of P}` is the **side-pairing**.
- Each ridge R (codimension-2 face) sits between two adjacent sides,
  and the sequence of sides one gets by repeatedly going to the
  "g_S-adjacent" side from a ridge closes after finitely many steps
  into a **cycle of sides** {S₁, …, S_ℓ}. The product
  `g_{S₁} g_{S₂} ⋯ g_{S_ℓ}` is the **cycle transformation** and has
  finite order k; the **cycle relation** is `(g_{S₁} ⋯ g_{S_ℓ})^k = 1`
  (Ratcliffe Theorem 6.8.7).
- Together with the side-pairing relations `g_S g_{S'} = 1`, the cycle
  relations form a complete presentation of Γ on the generators Φ
  (Poincaré's polygon theorem; Ratcliffe Theorem 13.5.3 for orbifolds,
  §11.2 for manifolds — same algebraic content).

Practical example: PSL(2,ℤ) acts on H² via Möbius, has a fundamental
polygon with three sides L, S, R (cf. Ratcliffe Figure 6.7.1). The
pairings are `g_R(z) = z + 1`, `g_S(z) = −1/z`, with `R' = L` and `S' = S`.
The cycle relations reduce to `(g_S g_R)³ = 1` and `g_S² = 1` — exactly
the PSL(2,ℤ) presentation `⟨R, S | R³, S²⟩` (after eliminating L = R⁻¹).

## Coxeter groups and reflection-group classification

A **reflection group** Γ ⊂ I(X) is one generated by reflections in the
sides of a polyhedron P. Ratcliffe Theorem 7.1.3: if P is finite-sided,
finite-volume, and **every dihedral angle is a submultiple of π**
(θ_ij = π/k_ij with k_ij ∈ ℤ≥₂ ∪ {∞}), then Γ is automatically discrete
with P as fundamental polyhedron. The presentation reduces to the
**Coxeter group**

    ⟨ S_i | S_i², (S_i S_j)^{k_ij} ⟩

(Ratcliffe Theorem 7.1.4). The **Coxeter graph** has the S_i as vertices
and an edge S_i—S_j labelled k_ij whenever k_ij > 2.

Classification of irreducible discrete reflection groups (Ratcliffe
Figs 7.2.7–7.2.9, simplex case):

| Space | Existence | Coxeter graphs |
|---|---|---|
| Spherical Sⁿ | finite list per n, all n | finite spherical Coxeter diagrams |
| Euclidean Eⁿ | finite list per n, all n | affine Coxeter diagrams (Ã, B̃, C̃, D̃, Ẽ, F̃, G̃) |
| Hyperbolic Hⁿ | only for n ≤ 4 (compact) or n ≤ 9 (non-compact with ideal vertices) | hyperbolic Coxeter diagrams |

In 2D specifically: the {p, q} regular tilings correspond to the
triangle reflection group T(2, p, q) — fundamental triangle with angles
π/2, π/p, π/q. Spherical iff `1/p + 1/q > 1/2` (the 5 Platonic
symmetries), Euclidean iff `= 1/2` (T(2,3,6), T(2,4,4), T(2,6,3)),
hyperbolic iff `< 1/2` (infinite family). The (2, 3, 7) hyperbolic
triangle is the smallest in area (the famous Klein-quartic geometry).

## How this maps to a renderer

To render a {p, q} tiling on the Poincaré disk, the operational steps are:

1. **Place one tile.** A regular p-gon at the disk centre — the centre of
   B² is the only point at which "hyperbolic angle = Euclidean angle" is
   visually free, so the central tile is easy to lay out.
2. **Enumerate the orbit.** Each generator of the reflection group is a
   reflection in a circle orthogonal to S¹. Compose generators in
   breadth-first order, applying each composition to the central tile to
   get an outer tile. Each isometry is one 2×2 matrix in PSL(2,ℝ) or
   PSU(1,1).
3. **Cull by depth.** The "size" of a tile in Euclidean screen
   coordinates shrinks geometrically with hyperbolic distance from the
   origin — after a few generations the tile is sub-pixel. Stop enumerating
   when the next tile's Euclidean diameter drops below a pixel.
4. **Draw the tile sides as circular arcs orthogonal to the unit
   circle.** A tile vertex v transforms by Möbius; tile sides are short
   geodesics, i.e. arcs orthogonal to S¹. Each side is one circular arc
   draw call (Bézier-approximated, or a custom shader).

This is the bog-standard *Circle Limit* approach and is what packages like
HyperRogue, KaleidoTile and Coxeter's own software do.

## What this maps to in the renderer

The repo's `Family` generators are Euclidean substitution / de-Bruijn
constructions; the projected vertices of any of them can be passed
through a radial homeomorphism ℝ² → B² and a τ_b boost to render in
the Poincaré disk — exactly what `projection-design.md` describes and
the shipping projection mode does. A {p, q} hyperbolic tiling is a
different beast: the orbit of a triangle reflection group T(2, p, q)
acting on H², built generator-side by a Coxeter / side-pairing
construction (Dirichlet domain + side-pairing → group presentation,
sections above). That generator path is its own `Family` subsystem,
distinct from the projection.
