# Heptagonal tilings — 7-fold

The 7-fold family. Hardest of the symmetry orders: 7-fold is impossible for
any periodic tiling and even the substitution rules are stubborn. Covered:
two naive heptagon attempts (no recurrence), the rhomb dualization tilings,
Savard's incomplete binary-style recurrence, and the two genuine published
substitution tilings (Harriss, Danzer).

For 7-fold the only attainable symmetry is **statistical** (limited-range);
exact symmetry can hold about at most one centre. The cut-and-project method
needs a **fractal acceptance domain** for 7-fold, which is why dualization is
preferred.

Source: `quadibloc.com/math/heptint.htm`.

---

## Keplerian heptagon tiling

**Summary.** The naive starting attempt — pack regular heptagons and fill the
gaps. No recurrence; a finite symmetric patch only.

| Property         | Value |
|------------------|-------|
| Symmetry order   | 7-fold (local cluster only) |
| Symmetry type    | single-centre (a finite symmetric patch) |
| Aperiodic        | no |
| Prototiles       | regular heptagon + interstitial gap shapes |
| Construction     | direct |
| Inflation factor | n/a |
| Attribution      | John Savard (in the spirit of Kepler) |

### Prototiles
Regular heptagons (interior angle 5·180°/7 ≈ 128.571°) plus interstitial
filler shapes, required because regular heptagons do not tile the plane.

### Construction
Direct construction — heptagons packed around a centre with gap fillers. Not
a substitution; the source explicitly notes that obtaining a true recurrence
is the hard part.

### Matching rules
None.

### Symmetry & aperiodicity
Not aperiodic — a finite 7-fold-symmetric arrangement of heptagons with
interstitial gaps.

### Variants & relations
Extends into the Dürer-type tiling (below).

### History & decoration
Inspired by Kepler's approach to polygon tilings.

### Renderer mapping
Not a `Family` — no recurrence to drive a generator. Documented to mark the
dead end.

### References
Johannes Kepler (spirit of); quadibloc `heptint.htm`.

## Dürer-type heptagonal tiling

**Summary.** Seven flattened-hexagon heptagon patches meeting at a 7-fold
centre — a *twinned* conventional symmetry, not an intrinsic one.

| Property         | Value |
|------------------|-------|
| Symmetry order   | 7-fold (twinned about one centre) |
| Symmetry type    | single-centre |
| Aperiodic        | no |
| Prototiles       | heptagonal shapes + interstitial fillers |
| Construction     | direct |
| Inflation factor | n/a |
| Attribution      | John Savard, after Albrecht Dürer |

### Prototiles
Heptagonal shapes plus the same interstitial fillers, arranged into seven
flattened-hexagon sub-tilings.

### Construction
Direct — seven hexagonal-type patches arranged around a 7-fold centre. No
recurrence.

### Matching rules
None.

### Symmetry & aperiodicity
Not aperiodic. The 7-fold symmetry is a *conventional symmetry twinned* about
one point — Savard calls this fundamentally trivial. The goal it falls short
of is a recurrence yielding *intrinsic* (statistical) global 7-fold symmetry.

### Variants & relations
An extension of the Keplerian heptagon attempt.

### History & decoration
Named for Albrecht Dürer, who constructed heptagon-based patterns.

### Renderer mapping
Not a `Family` — twinned-symmetry construction, no generative rule.

### References
Albrecht Dürer; quadibloc `heptint.htm`.

## Rhomb dualization heptagonal tilings

**Summary.** The genuine 7-fold tilings produced by de Bruijn's dualization
(grid) method — built from the three 7-fold rhombs.

| Property         | Value |
|------------------|-------|
| Symmetry order   | 7-fold |
| Symmetry type    | statistical |
| Aperiodic        | yes (quasiperiodic) |
| Prototiles       | 3 rhombs with narrow angles 180°/7, 360°/7, 540°/7 |
| Construction     | dualization |
| Inflation factor | n/a |
| Attribution      | de Bruijn dualization method |

### Prototiles
Three rhombs — the distinct rhombi whose angles are multiples of 180°/7:
narrow angles ≈ 25.7° (180°/7), ≈ 51.4° (360°/7), ≈ 77.1° (540°/7). Two
composite shapes are drawn from these rhombs: a **symmetric** 7-fold rosette
cluster, and an **asymmetric** larger cluster (optionally with an outer shell
of thin rhombs). The asymmetric shape *without* its outer shell is the one
actually found in dualization-generated heptagonal tilings.

### Construction
Generated by the dualization / grid method, not by substitution. The
cut-and-project method would need a *fractal* acceptance domain for 7-fold;
dualization produces 7-fold tilings as readily as any other symmetry.

### Matching rules
None — generated by dualization.

### Symmetry & aperiodicity
Quasiperiodic, statistical 7-fold. Dualization tilings have spaces suitable
for inserting the symmetric rosette as well as the standard asymmetric
cluster.

### Variants & relations
The asymmetric shape with vs. without its thin-rhomb outer shell. These
tilings motivate the substitution attempts below.

### History & decoration
Quasicrystal-research context; dualization is de Bruijn's method.

### Renderer mapping
Implemented — `Family::Heptagonal`. Generated by `generateMultigrid(7, …)` in
`tiling/penrose.cpp`: the de Bruijn dualization of seven line grids yields the
three 7-fold rhombs (`type` 0/1/2). The ripple shader uses `waveSymmetry` 14.
It shares the multigrid generator with the dodecagonal and Ammann–Beenker
families — only the grid count differs.

### References
N. G. de Bruijn, "Algebraic theory of Penrose's non-periodic tilings of the
plane", *Nederl. Akad. Wetensch. Proc. Ser. A* **84** (1981) 38–66 (the
dualization method, here run on seven line grids); quadibloc `heptint.htm`.

## Savard's binary-style heptagonal recurrence

**Summary.** An attempted 7-fold substitution inspired by the rhomb shapes
above and the binary-tiling principle. **Incomplete** — only half the tile
orientations are solved.

| Property         | Value |
|------------------|-------|
| Symmetry order   | 7-fold (intended) |
| Symmetry type    | statistical (intended) |
| Aperiodic        | yes (intended) |
| Prototiles       | the three 7-fold rhombs, each in two border-direction orientations |
| Construction     | substitution (incomplete) |
| Inflation factor | not stated |
| Attribution      | John Savard |

### Prototiles
The 7-fold rhombs (three sizes — small green, blue, yellow in the source
legend). Each rhomb type comes in **two orientations**, distinguished by the
direction its borders run, colour-coded in the diagram.

### Construction
Shows one-generation recurrences for rhombs whose borders run in **one**
direction (colour-coded blue and green): each parent rhomb expands into a
cluster of smaller rhombs (worked patches: a vertical lens, a horizontal
lens, a diamond-ish expansion), with thin uncoloured outline tiles indicating
overlap context.

### Matching rules
Border-running direction is the operative orientation rule; colour-coded
(blue vs purple, green vs blue-green).

### Symmetry & aperiodicity
Intended to be an aperiodic statistical-7-fold substitution tiling.

### Variants & relations
- **Why incomplete** — defining recurrences for the *other* border direction
  (the would-be purple and blue-green tiles) runs into unsolved problems.
- **Workaround** — chop the rhombs in half into triangles; then border
  direction no longer matters for the recurrence, sidestepping the unsolved
  cases. This produces a working heptagonal tiling at the cost of introducing
  triangles.

### History & decoration
Follows the binary-tiling principle (see `pentagonal-binary.md`).

### Renderer mapping
Not implemented — and not implementable as published; the recurrence is
incomplete. The chop-into-triangles workaround *is* generative and could in
principle drive a `Family`, but Harriss's tiling (below) is the better target.

### References
quadibloc `heptint.htm`.

## Harriss / Goodman-Strauss n-fold rhomb substitution

**Summary.** A rhomb-only substitution tiling with order-n rotational
symmetry. Goodman-Strauss devised the n=7 rule; Harriss generalised it to
every n. The first proof that rhomb substitution tilings exist with scaling
factors of arbitrarily high algebraic degree — beyond what cut-and-project
can reach.

| Property         | Value |
|------------------|-------|
| Symmetry order   | 7-fold (the tiling space is closed under order-14 rotation) |
| Symmetry type    | statistical (single-centre order-7 for the singular seeds) |
| Aperiodic        | yes |
| Prototiles       | 3 — the 7-fold rhombs T₁, T₂, T₃ (plus marked variants R, P, Q) |
| Construction     | substitution (non-volume-hierarchic) |
| Inflation factor | 2 + 2·cos(π/7) ≈ 3.801937 |
| Attribution      | Chaim Goodman-Strauss (n=7 rule); Edmund Harriss (general n) |

### Prototiles
The three rhombs of unit edge whose vertex angles are integer multiples of
π/7. Writing T_k for the rhomb with the two angle values kπ/7 and (7−k)π/7:
- **T₁** — π/7 (≈25.714°) / 6π/7 (≈154.286°): a thin sliver.
- **T₂** — 2π/7 (≈51.429°) / 5π/7 (≈128.571°): a medium rhomb.
- **T₃** — 3π/7 (≈77.143°) / 4π/7 (≈102.857°): a fat, near-square rhomb.

T_k and T_{7−k} are the same shape, so k ∈ {1,2,3} exhausts the prototiles.
The substitution additionally distinguishes *marked* copies of these shapes
— R, P, Q (see Construction) — that carry different edge decorations and
substitute differently, so a complete rule tracks six labels, not three
shapes. Construction convention: build on the seven unit directions
e_j = (cos(jπ/7), sin(jπ/7)), j = 0..6; a rhomb is the unit parallelogram
spanned by two of them, and its type k is their index difference folded
into {1,2,3}.

### Construction
A primitive substitution: the inflated rhomb is dissected into smaller
rhombs of the same three shapes. The linear inflation factor is

    λ = 2 + 2·cos(π/7) = 3.801937…

derived from the edge rule — one unit edge is replaced by two parallel unit
edges plus two unit edges turned by ±π/7, total length 2 + 2·cos(π/7). λ is
a degree-3 algebraic integer (a Perron number); its conjugates are
2 + 2·cos(3π/7) ≈ 2.445 and 2 + 2·cos(5π/7) ≈ 0.753. A conjugate exceeds 1,
so λ is **not** a Pisot number — which is why (Harriss) these tilings cannot
be produced by cut-and-project for n ≥ 7.

The substitution is **not volume-hierarchic**: the children do not fill a
clean enlarged rhomb. Each edge of the inflated rhomb is distorted by a
central "dimple" — a V-notch of apex angle π/7 — pointing alternately inward
and outward around the rhomb (which is why T_k and T_{7−k}, once marked,
differ). The marked tiles are **R** (the dimples differ between the two
pairs of opposite edges), **P** (three dimples out, one in) and **Q** (three
in, one out). The dissection is fixed uniquely by two constraints: every
child edge obeys the same dimple rule, and the rhomb angles meeting at each
interior vertex sum to 2π.

Per-parent child counts (Harriss, Table 1). Master row, 2 ≤ p ≤ n−2: a rhomb
T_p expands to 6·T_p + 1·T_{p−2} + 1·T_{p+2} + 4·T_{n−p−1} + 4·T_{n−p+1}.
For n=7 the thin-rhomb row is T₁ → 5·T₁ + 1·T₃ + 4·T₂ (ten children). The
one-generation expansions of T₁, T₂, T₃ are the three patches in the source
rule diagram.

### Matching rules
None are needed to *generate* the tiling — it is a substitution. The dimpled
edges function as a built-in matching rule that keeps the children packed
without overlap (being non-volume-hierarchic, the rule could not pack a plain
rhomb). Dimple apex angle π/7; the inward/outward assignment alternates
around each rhomb.

### Symmetry & aperiodicity
The translational local-isomorphism class of the substitution is closed
under rotation by π/7 — order-14 (2n) rotational symmetry of the tiling
*space* (statistical symmetry). Generic individual tilings have no exact
rotation centre. Seeding the substitution with the thin rhomb T₁ arranged
around a point produces a *singular* tiling with exact order-7 rotational
symmetry about that point; seeding with T₅ (= T₂) gives order-7 without
mirror symmetry. The substitution is primitive — iteration carries every
tile to every tile in every orientation — and λ is an algebraic Perron
number that is not a root of unity, so no tiling it produces is periodic.

### Variants & relations
Goodman-Strauss gave the rule only for n=7 (unpublished). Harriss generalised
it to every n: rhombs with angles kπ/n, a substitution closed under order-2n
rotation, inflation 2 + 2·cos(π/n). n=4 reuses the Ammann–Beenker rhombs but
does not generate Ammann–Beenker tilings; n=5 likewise differs from Penrose.
The n → n+2 rules form one continuous family ("rods joined by hinges").
Contrast the de Bruijn rhomb dualization heptagonal tilings above — the same
rhomb shapes obtained by projection, with quadratic scaling; Harriss's are
substitution tilings with higher-degree scaling, provably outside
cut-and-project for n ≥ 7. A separate, later construction (Maloney 2014)
reaches n-fold substitution tilings by a different route (Kannan–Soroker
polygon tiling plus Kenyon's method).

### History & decoration
Chaim Goodman-Strauss devised the n=7 rhomb substitution and communicated it
privately; it was never published, surviving only as a figure reproduced in
Harriss's work. Edmund Harriss generalised it to arbitrary n in his Imperial
College PhD thesis (2003) and in *Discrete & Computational Geometry* 34
(2005) 523–536.

### Renderer mapping
Not implemented, and not implementable from the documented data alone. The
sources fix the prototiles, the inflation factor λ = 2 + 2·cos(π/7), and the
exact per-parent child *counts* (Harriss Table 1) — but they give the actual
dissection only as figures (Harriss Fig. 2 and 7–9), never as child
coordinates. Because the substitution is not volume-hierarchic, the children
fill a dimpled region rather than a plain rhomb, so the area-conservation and
gap/overlap-closure checks that verify the renderer's other substitutions
(P1, P2, P3, pinwheel, Tübingen, binary) do not apply directly. A faithful
`Family` would require: digitising Harriss Fig. 2 into a child list of
(label, edge-direction pair, translation, orientation); tracking six labels
(T₁, T₂, T₃ and the marked R, P, Q); modelling the π/7 dimple on every edge;
and verifying each patch against the 2π interior-vertex condition. That
reconstruction is a research task in its own right — deferred until the child
geometry can be pinned down and verified on-device. `waveSymmetry` would
be 7.

### References
- E. O. Harriss, "Non-Periodic Rhomb Substitution Tilings that Admit Order n
  Rotational Symmetry," *Discrete & Computational Geometry* **34** (2005)
  523–536 — the source paper; gives the rule as Fig. 2, no child coordinates.
- C. Goodman-Strauss, the n=7 rhomb substitution (personal communication;
  reproduced as Fig. 2 of Harriss 2005).
- C. Goodman-Strauss, "Matching rules and substitution tilings," *Annals of
  Mathematics* **147** (1998) 181–223.
- G. R. Maloney, "On substitution tilings of the plane with n-fold rotational
  symmetry," arXiv:1409.1828 (2014).
- Tilings Encyclopedia (D. Frettlöh, E. Harriss, F. Gähler),
  `tilings.math.uni-bielefeld.de` — carries the worked rule diagram and patch;
  the practical resource for reconstructing the child geometry.
- Note: Harriss's PhD thesis *On Canonical Substitution Tilings* (2003) does
  **not** contain this substitution — it treats cut-and-project (canonical)
  substitution tilings, a separate family; see `octagonal.md`.

## Danzer sevenfold tiling

**Summary.** The first substitution tiling achieved with 7-fold symmetry;
triangle-based.

| Property         | Value |
|------------------|-------|
| Symmetry order   | 7-fold |
| Symmetry type    | statistical |
| Aperiodic        | yes |
| Prototiles       | triangles (angles multiples of 180°/7) |
| Construction     | substitution |
| Inflation factor | not stated |
| Attribution      | L. Danzer |

### Prototiles
Triangle-based (not rhombs). The specific triangles are not detailed in the
source; for 7-fold they have angles that are multiples of 180°/7.

### Construction
A substitution tiling — historically the first one achieved for 7-fold
symmetry — built on triangle prototiles. The explicit rule is not reproduced
in the source.

### Matching rules
None stated.

### Symmetry & aperiodicity
Aperiodic substitution tiling; historically the first sevenfold substitution
tiling.

### Variants & relations
Danzer's best-known tiling is a different, three-dimensional one with
icosahedral symmetry; this heptagonal tiling is noted as his first sevenfold
substitution tiling.

### History & decoration
L. Danzer.

### Renderer mapping
Not implemented. A triangle-based 7-fold `Family` candidate, but the source
gives no explicit rule — implementation would require the rule from Danzer's
own work, not these PDFs. Behind Harriss as a 7-fold target.

### References
L. Danzer; quadibloc `heptint.htm`.
