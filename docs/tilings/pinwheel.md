# Pinwheel tiling — non-rotational substitution

The Conway–Radin pinwheel: a single right triangle whose substitution drives
its tiles into infinitely many orientations, so the tiling has no rotational
symmetry of any finite order. It is the renderer's fifth `Family`.

Source: `quadibloc.com/math/til04.htm`; the substitution geometry is from the
primary literature (C. Radin, *Annals of Mathematics* 139, 1994).

---

## Conway–Radin pinwheel

**Summary.** A single 1:2:√5 right triangle that substitutes into five copies
of itself at 1/√5 scale; because the substitution turns by an irrational
angle, tiles appear in infinitely many orientations — the first aperiodic
prototile set proven to need rotations, not just translations, to describe
its tilings.

| Property         | Value |
|------------------|-------|
| Symmetry order   | none — tiles occur in infinitely many orientations |
| Symmetry type    | statistical |
| Aperiodic        | yes |
| Prototiles       | 1 — a 1:2:√5 right triangle (both chiralities used) |
| Construction     | substitution |
| Inflation factor | √5 |
| Attribution      | J. H. Conway (tessellation); Charles Radin (forced tiling) |

### Prototiles
One right triangle with legs 1 and 2 and hypotenuse √5. Its three interior
angles are all distinct — small **S** = arctan(1/2) ≈ 26.57°, right **L** =
90°, medium **M** = arctan(2) ≈ 63.43° — so the three vertices and three edges
are each labelled S, M, L by size. Both the triangle and its mirror image
occur. Radin's marked prototiles add vertex "marks" that encode the
hierarchy, making the bare triangle a genuine aperiodic set; the tiling itself
is generated by the inflation rule alone.

### Construction
One deflation replaces the triangle by **five** triangles, each similar to it
at scale 1/√5. Placing the parent as Radin's canonical level-1 triangle with
vertices (−2,1), (2,−1), (3,1), the five children are:
- **A** — (−2,1), (0,1), (0,0)
- **B** — (0,1), (0,0), (2,1)
- **C** — (0,0), (2,0), (2,1) — same placement as the unscaled prototile
- **D** — (0,0), (2,0), (2,−1)
- **E** — (2,1), (3,1), (2,−1)

The parent triangle has sides √5 : 2√5 : 5 — exactly √5 times the prototile —
so each child is prototile-sized. Two of the five (A and B) are mirror images
of the other three. The substitution carries the small angle S through a turn
of arctan(1/2); since arctan(1/2) is an irrational multiple of π (Niven's
theorem), iterating it never returns to a previous orientation.

### Matching rules
None. The pinwheel is a pure substitution tiling. Radin's paper does supply an
equivalent finite **marked** prototile set whose matching rules force the
hierarchy — that is what proves the pinwheel a genuine aperiodic set — but the
tiling is built by the inflation rule above, not by edge markings.

### Symmetry & aperiodicity
Aperiodic — no periodic tiling is possible. Uniquely among the classical
aperiodic sets, the pinwheel's tiles occur in **infinitely many
orientations**: the tiling has no rotational symmetry of any finite order, and
its tilings can only be analysed using the full Euclidean group, not just the
translation subgroup. Any finite patch still recurs, in every orientation,
infinitely often, so the tiling is statistically isotropic — "round" — with a
circularly symmetric diffraction spectrum.

### Variants & relations
- **Sadun's generalisation** — the construction works for other right
  triangles if tiles of more than one size are admitted in a single tiling;
  the 3:4:5 triangle is the standard example. Right triangles whose acute
  angles *are* rational multiples of a circle then yield substitution tilings
  of any prescribed finite rotational symmetry.
- The pinwheel is the planar model for Radin's 3-D "quaquaversal" tiling.

### History & decoration
The substitution tessellation is due to John H. Conway; Charles Radin (1994)
turned it into a forced aperiodic prototile set and named it for the pinwheel
toy, whose blades rotate about a stick. It is the standard textbook example of
a tiling with a circularly symmetric diffraction pattern.

### Renderer mapping
Implemented — `Family::Pinwheel` in
`android/app/src/main/cpp/tiling/penrose.cpp`. Each tile is a triangle stored
as its [S, L, M] corners; `subdividePinwheel` applies the five-child rule
above through the unique affine map carrying the canonical parent triangle
onto the stored one, which reproduces reflected tiles without special
handling. `type` records chirality (0/1) and feeds the two-bucket "type"
colouring. Seeds: a square (four triangles), a single triangle, and a 2:1
rectangle. Generation is capped at 6 — growth is 5× per step. The ripple
shader uses an isotropic radial wave for this family: the pinwheel has no
preferred axis, so the directional plane-wave sums used by the 5/4/12-fold
families do not apply.
